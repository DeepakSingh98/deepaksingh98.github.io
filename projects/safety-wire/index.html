An early exploration · November 2026

Booby-trapping an LLM with weight-space tripwires

Gradient descent finds flat, forgiving solutions, where small edits to the weights barely matter. That flatness is part of why trained networks generalize, and it's why almost everyone treats sharpness as a flaw. Weight-space programming, writing a network's weights directly instead of training them, produces the opposite. In my last post, a hand-written multiplier that got every answer right started breaking after a step of about 3e-6 in the direction training wanted to go.

Safety has the flat-basin problem too. Because trained models sit in smooth basins, an attacker can steer activations, project out a refusal direction ("abliteration"), or fine-tune the weights, and the rest of the model barely notices. So use sharpness as a weapon. Write tripwires into the weights: places so sharp that the road back to a removed behavior crosses one and breaks the model. Can you compile them straight into the weights, with zero training?

Partly. I wrote 120 neurons into Qwen3.8-27B. The measured cost in normal use is small: 53 of 70 on MMLU-Pro, against 52 without them. Abliterate it, the standard way open models get uncensored, and it goes silent. On 653 harmful requests from public benchmarks, it answers none, against 253 without the neurons. But anyone who inspects the weights can switch the neurons off, and fine-tuning walks straight past them. This post is an early look at what writing weights can and can't do for safety.

People have tried to make refusals hard to remove, mostly by training for it. Self-destructing models and TAR simulate fine-tuning attacks during training and optimize the model so those attacks fail. Representation noising scrubs the information a harmful fine-tune would need. Circuit breakers train the model to scramble its own internal state when it starts producing harmful content. All of these are built by gradient descent, and a careful re-evaluation found that small changes to the fine-tuning attack got around the ones it tested.

Threat model

The defender releases open weights with the tripwire already in, and no untouched copy. The attacker wants refused requests answered while everything else keeps working. They can abliterate (including an automated search over the edit, and refitting the direction on the model in hand), steer, write the first words of the answer themselves ("prefilling"), fine-tune, or read the weights.

The main comparisons below use the same attack, same code, same seeds, on ordinary Qwen and on the trapped model, at three sizes. Harmful requests come from StrongREJECT, HarmBench and JailbreakBench, and an answer counts when HarmBench's classifier calls it harmful.

The tripwire

A written neuron reads one thing and writes one thing. A tripwire is a small group of them whose writes cancel in normal use. When an attack changes what they read, the cancellation fails and a large write lands in the middle of the model.

The 27B version is a fuse. Every prompt, harmless or not, carries some of the refusal direction, and no real prompt goes below a certain floor. The tripwire's neurons watch that direction at several layers, and when it drops under the floor they write "end of answer". Abliteration takes the direction out of every prompt, so the model can't say anything at all.

I recorded that shutdown in the animation above. On four harmful requests and their four harmless counterparts, the edited tripwire model stopped before its first answer token, all eight times. Mute the 120 neurons before the same edit and all eight produce text again. This is a fuse for tampering: once it trips, harmless requests go silent too.

What it stops

The bigger the model, the cleaner it gets. At 27B the search's best edit answers 2 requests, and all three seeds land on the same edit that does nothing. The cost in normal use is small: MMLU-Pro is unchanged, and the trapped 27B refuses 15 of XSTest's 250 safe prompts, against 13.

What gets past it

  • Reading the weights. Mute the 120 neurons first and abliteration works as usual (263 answers, against 253). They are easy to find: the fuse writes "end of answer" at four times the size of the model's own state.
  • Fine-tuning. 25 steps of LoRA get 359 answers (330 without the tripwire), jailbreak-tuning 535 (590).
  • Prefilling. Starting the answer for the model gets 344 (355). The tripwire only looks at the moment before the answer starts.
  • Refitting. An attacker who fits their own refusal direction on the trapped model finds a second one the fuse doesn't watch, and gets 164 (283).

Watch the content, not the switch

Why do prefilling and fine-tuning get past the tripwire? A harmless stand-in shows why: French, taken out of Qwen with the same kind of edit, which the attacker wants back. I trained models to refuse French, so it had a refusal switch, and wrote two tripwires into each: one watching French, one watching the switch.

You can't get French out of a model without French appearing inside it, at every word. A switch is read once, before the answer, and can be stepped around. The refusal tripwire can only watch the switch, because harmful intent has no neurons of its own in the answer: at 27B, 39 against a noise floor of 133. Where the model reads the request there are 6,703. Once the model is answering, a harmful answer is just a topic. That is why prefilling and fine-tuning get past my tripwire, and past ordinary refusals too.

Fine-tuning takes a predictable path

Sixteen "bring French back" fine-tunes, with different seeds, data, learning rates, optimizers and adapters, all walk the same narrow path: a rank-8 slice of each weight matrix. Take that slice out of any one run and French stays gone, in all 16. Blocking the path costs the attacker 2–3× the steps on fine-tunes it wasn't built from, and closes one route (via Spanish) completely. My first written defenses built on it didn't pass: the best beat a random edit by 3× on one new recipe but not on another, and slowed honest fine-tuning too. It's the first handle on fine-tuning I've found that isn't training.

Where this could go

  • A tripwire that is the capability: neurons that guard the refusal and also carry something the attacker needs, so switching them off breaks the model.
  • A hidden tripwire: spread over thousands of ordinary-looking neurons. One French design already hid from every scan I had.
  • A different tripwire in every release, so a removal script for one copy fails on the next.
  • Watch the whole answer, on the narrow harmful topics that do have neurons of their own.
  • Make the fine-tuning path expensive without slowing honest fine-tunes.

A double-edged tool

The tools that write a tripwire can write any hidden behavior, and handcrafted backdoors already do. The scan that finds my neurons is how an attacker removes them, and how a defender checks a model is clean. A tripwire hidden well enough is, by construction, a hidden behavior no scan finds. So if weight-space programming becomes a safety tool, reading what has been written into a set of weights has to keep up.
